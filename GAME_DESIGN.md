# The Forest Eats Memories — Game Design Document

---

## 1. Concept

A 2D story-driven exploration game set in a fictional rural Japanese-inspired village in the early 1990s.
Near the village sits an ancient forest that people visit when they are in pain.
They always come back happy.
They always come back emptier.

The game asks: **what is a memory worth?**

---

## 2. Tone & Atmosphere

The feeling is more important than any plot point.

| Quality       | Expression in game                                        |
|---------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Nostalgia     | 1994 setting, analogue sounds, warm interiors             |
| Quiet mystery | Long pauses, unfinished sentences, things half-seen       |
| Sadness       | Kenta, the father's absence, the grandmother's stories    |
| Comfort       | The shop owner, the summer house, fireflies at dusk       |
| Wonder        | Forest creatures, strange phenomena, the children's art   |

**Not**: horror. Not spooky. Melancholic but never dark in a threatening way.

Closest references:  
- *My Neighbor Totoro* (rural magic, childhood, nature)  
- *Only Yesterday* (memory, nostalgia, countryside)  
- *Night in the Woods* (quiet small town, emotional realism)  
- *Spirited Away* (strange beings who misunderstand humans)  

---

## 3. The Core Tension

The forest creatures are not evil. This is the hardest thing for players to hold.

They are ancient. They perceive human suffering as a weight, a fog, something they can carry instead.  
They do not understand that the memories they consume — grief, guilt, longing — are load-bearing walls.  
Remove them and the person still stands. But the house is wrong. Hollow.

The game never resolves this into a simple moral.  
The creatures are genuinely helping, the way a child helps by hiding something they think is upsetting you.

## 4. Characters

### Hiro (the player)
Age 10–11. Curious, observant, slightly lonely.  
His father is absent — it becomes clear he hasn't come on this trip, and hasn't been around for a while.  
Hiro notices things adults dismiss. He draws what he sees.  
He is not brave. He is just *there*, and paying attention.

### Mom (Yuki)
30s. Tired under her warmth. She chose this house because she knows it — she grew up nearby.  
She avoids the subject of Dad, the subject of the forest, the subject of the future.  
She loves Hiro but she's holding something back.  
*Later:* We learn she once entered the forest as a teenager. She doesn't remember why she did.

### Harue (shop owner)
60s. Round. Warm. Knows everything. Says almost nothing directly.  
She has watched the village change over decades. She is sad in a way that has become comfortable.  
She will tell Hiro stories if he asks the right questions.

### Kenta
40s. Grey coat. Empty eyes. Moves like he's underwater.  
He was a carpenter. He had a wife who died. He entered the forest last spring.  
He came back happy. He cannot remember his wife's name.  
He smiles at strangers now. He has no idea why people avoid him.  
*The horror:* he is not suffering. He seems fine. The loss is invisible to him.

### Grandma Sachi (Yuki's mother, met mid-game)
70s. Lives in the village. Sharp and careful.  
She knows the oldest stories about the forest. She calls the creatures *the listeners*.  
She is the only adult who will speak plainly to Hiro.  
She tells him: "They cannot give back what they take. That is not cruelty. That is just what they are."

### Riku (village boy)
9. Lives near the forest. Won't go anywhere near it.  
He has drawings — detailed, disturbing drawings of creatures he claims he's never seen.  
He and Hiro become friends. He is a counterpoint: where Hiro is curious, Riku is afraid.

---

## 5. The Creatures

All creatures are forest-born. They are not malevolent. They are strange and old.

### Design principles
- Soft, round forms — nothing sharp or predatory
- Made of natural material: moss, bark, fog, rain, leaves, soil
- Large eyes — the most expressive feature, like Totoro
- They move in unexpected ways: sinking into ground, drifting upward, disappearing mid-stride
- They carry things: small lights, bundled objects, tied pouches

### Named Creatures

**The Listener**  
The first one Hiro sees. Small — cat-sized. Looks like a bundle of dark moss with two pale green eyes.  
It runs away when seen. Later, Hiro learns these are the ones who approach grieving humans.  
They sit quietly beside a person and wait. Eventually the person leans against them, without knowing why.  
And something passes between them.

**The Keeper**  
Much larger — house-sized when fully seen. Rarely moves. Found deep in the forest.  
Round, ancient, like a hill that breathes. The Keeper holds what the Listeners collect.  
Hiro never speaks to the Keeper. He sees it once, from a distance. That is enough.

**The Thread-Walkers**  
Tall, thin, made of something like woven fog. Walk in single file along paths only they can see.  
They do not interact with humans. They seem to be mapping, or measuring, or remembering something else.

**The Pale**  
Small. Look like folded paper, or white leaves. Float just above the ground.  
They appear around people who have been touched by the Listener — drawn to the absence.  
Hiro can see them. Most adults cannot.

## 6. Story Structure

### Chapter 1 — Arrived
The drive. The village. The house. The first glimpse.  
Establish: the summer, the quiet, the absence of Dad, the first shadow of Kenta.  
End beat: the word "went." Hiro begins to understand something is wrong.

### Chapter 2 — The Village Lives
Hiro explores. He meets Riku. He finds Riku's drawings.  
He sees an old woman enter the forest — and comes back the next morning, peaceful, calling her daughter by the wrong name.  
He hears the festival preparations. He learns the festival involves an offering to the forest.  
Grandma Sachi appears. She gives him the vocabulary: *the listeners, the keeper, the pale.*

### Chapter 3 — What Dad Left
Hiro finds a photo in the summer house. His father, young, standing at the forest edge.  
He asks his mom. She says nothing. He asks Grandma Sachi.  
She says: "Your father came here the summer before you were born. He was very unhappy then."  
"Did he go in?"  
Long silence.  
"Part of him did."

### Chapter 4 — Into the Listening
Hiro enters the forest edge — not deep, just the border. He meets a Listener.  
No speech. They sit together. The Listener does not take from him.  
It is confused. It sits beside him and waits and eventually it seems to understand:  
*this one's grief is not ready to be carried. it is still growing into something.*  
The Listener leaves. Hiro watches it go.

### Chapter 5 — The Festival
The village festival. Lanterns on the river. The offering — a box of small written papers, each one a bad memory someone wants to release.  
The box goes into the forest. The children watch from a distance.  
Riku grabs Hiro's arm. "They're here. They're watching us."  
Hiro looks. In the treeline: dozens of pale green lights.  
More than he's ever seen.

### Chapter 6 — Kenta, Again
Two weeks later. Kenta passes Hiro on the street.  
He has started smiling more. He volunteers at the school. He laughs at things.  
He stops Hiro.  
"You gave me back my coins once. I remember that."  
"...Yes."  
"I don't remember much. But I remember that."  
He smiles. Walks away.  
Hiro stands there for a long time.

### Ending (multiple outcomes based on choice)

**A — Leave it alone.**  
Hiro goes home at the end of summer. He draws all the creatures in his notebook.  
He watches his mom carefully. She seems a little lighter here. He doesn't ask why.  
He comes back the next summer. And the summer after.  
The Listener is there at the forest edge. Waiting, as always.

**B — The question.**  
Before leaving, Hiro brings a piece of paper to the forest edge.  
He writes on it: *Why?*  
He leaves it there and walks away.  
The next morning it is gone. In its place: a bundle of dark moss, tied with something like a reed bow.  
He takes it home. He keeps it his whole life.  
He never finds out if it was an answer or just a gift.

**C — Let Mom go.**  
Hiro, at the end, understands that his Mom wants to go in.  
He doesn't stop her. He doesn't know how.  
She goes in on the last evening.  
She comes back before dark. She is lighter. She reaches out and holds his hand.  
She calls him by name.  
On the drive home she hums something. He realises he has never heard her hum before.  
He does not ask what she gave away.  
He watches the forest shrink in the side mirror.  
And feels grief for something he cannot name.

## 7. Gameplay Mechanics

### No combat. No fail states.

The game cannot be lost. It can only be experienced more or less fully.

### Exploration
Hiro walks, and the world responds.  
Wind chimes when he passes them. A cat that follows for half a screen then doesn't.  
Fireflies at dusk. A vending machine that still sells something from five years ago.  
Not interactive in a mechanical sense — interactive in the sense that being present matters.

### Observation Mode
Hold still for 3 seconds: the world slows slightly. Ambient details sharpen.  
A frog on a stone. Steam from a grate. A kite caught in a tree. A woman watching from a window who looks away.  
None of these unlock anything. They are just things that are true.

### Dialogue choices
When Hiro speaks, the player chooses between 2–3 options.  
None are right or wrong. They change what Hiro says, what people say back, what he notices.  
Some choices are remembered — Harue treats you differently if you asked about Kenta early.

### The Notebook
Hiro can draw what he sees. This is optional.  
The drawings are simple — the game draws for you based on what he's observed.  
At the end of each chapter, the notebook is shown. It is a record of attention.  
Players who used Observation Mode more have richer notebook pages.

### Chapter transitions
Always: fade to black. A line of narration. Then the next scene.  
Never cutscenes with voice acting. The narration is in second-person prose:  
*"You carry the bag of groceries back through the village. It is heavier than you expected."*

## 8. Environmental Storytelling

Things that should be true without being explained:

- The village shop has a shelf of photo albums for sale. Most are already partially filled.
- Kenta's house has flower boxes on every window. The flowers are all dead now.
- The festival offering box is too large for one year's worth of grief.
- There is a path into the forest that has been worn smooth by feet, but the grass grows back immediately around it.
- Children's drawings pinned to the school fence show creatures with large eyes. When you ask the teacher about them she says "the children have good imaginations."
- An old man in the village can tell you the name of every family who has lived in every house. There are gaps. He doesn't explain the gaps.
- The train that passes twice a day always slows near the forest. Just slightly.

## 9. Art Direction

### Style
2D. Side-scrolling with occasional vertical movement.  
Silhouette-based backgrounds — deep layers of shadow with warm light sources.  
Characters are small against the world (Ghibli scale — the environment is the protagonist).  
Rain, wind, and natural movement are constant.

### Colour palette

| Context      | Colours                                                |
|--------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Sky/night    | Deep indigo-black → slate-teal at horizon              |
| Forest       | Near-black greens, charcoal, occasional pale glow      |
| Village      | Warm amber windows against dark silhouette buildings   |
| Daytime      | Muted greens, grey sky, wet stone                      |
| The creatures | Soft greens, faint white, forest shadows               |

### Character scale
- Hiro: ~40px on a 960×540 canvas (about 1/24 of screen width)
- Small against wide horizons — like a child really is
- Slightly larger during close dialogue moments (implied zoom)

### Lighting
Every scene has a light source that matters:  
- Rain scenes: cold ambient + warm window spill  
- Forest scenes: darkness with small green glows (eyes, spirits)  
- Shop: warm overhead lamp, dust motes  
- Festival: lantern light on water

## 10. Sound Design

All procedurally generated or sourced from royalty-free ambience.

| Moment                  | Sound                                               |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| Opening                 | Rain on metal, windshield wipers, distant thunder   |
| Car ride                | Rain, tyres on wet road, engine hum                 |
| Village                 | Wind, rain on roofs, distant cicadas, wind chimes   |
| Forest edge             | Cicadas, a single frog, the rain stopping           |
| Forest interior         | Silence. Then: a low pulse. Then: silence again.    |
| Creature appears        | No sound. Or: the absence of the ambient layer.     |
| Kenta's coin drop       | Scattered coins — then silence                      |
| Festival                | Taiko drums far away, voices, lantern paper flutter |
| End credits             | Rain starting again, distant train                  |

**The sound design rule:** the creatures are louder in their absence.  
When a creature is near, one ambient layer always fades slightly.  
The world holds its breath.

## 11. Pacing

### Rhythm within scenes
Long → short → long.  
A wide establishing shot (atmospheric, slow).  
Then a short interaction (dialogue, observation, a thing noticed).  
Then silence / walking / the world returning.

### Chapter rhythm
Chapter 1: wonder, arrival  
Chapter 2: curiosity, expansion  
Chapter 3: the first real crack  
Chapter 4: direct encounter  
Chapter 5: scale — the world is bigger than Hiro  
Chapter 6: the cost, made personal  
End: a held breath  

### Dialogue pacing
Typewriter text. Slightly slower than you'd expect.  
Pauses are actual pauses — not just empty lines but a visible `...` held on screen.  
Characters like Kenta have much slower typewriter speed (his thoughts are slower now).

---

## 12. What the Game Is Not

- Not a horror game. Never cheap scares.
- Not a puzzle game. There are no locked doors that require specific items.
- Not a morality system. Choices shape experience, not outcomes.
- Not about saving anyone. You cannot rescue Kenta. You cannot stop the forest. The question is what you do with that.
- Not explained. The game never tells you what the creatures are, where they came from, or what happens to the memories they take. Some questions are for the player to hold, not answer.
